Purpose and Scope 
 
The purpose of this document is to clearly outline the operational workflows, case definitions, 
classification criteria, and analytic processes used to identify, classify, and report heat-related 
illness (morbidity) and death (mortality) in Maricopa County. It is designed to serve as a reference 
for a diverse audience, including Maricopa County Department of Public Health (MCDPH) staff, 
epidemiologists, public health professionals, external partners, and policymakers seeking 
replicable or adaptable models for their jurisdictions.  
While it provides a brief historical context, its primary emphasis is on current practice. This 
document is not a seasonal summary, formal protocol, or operational guide, nor does it represent 
methods used by the Maricopa County Office of the Medical Examiner or address internal 
infrastructure or funding. This document also mentions but does not cover in detail the 
production of our annual and multi-year reports. 
  
Recommendations for Use 
 
Heat surveillance data collected through the methodology outlined in this paper are used to support multiple applications, including the generation of internal and external dashboards, weekly and annual reports, and annual heat response operations, including activation of the Incident Command System. In addition, the data are used for statistical modeling and spatial analysis to support real-time public health decision-making during periods of extreme heat. Although the methods outlined are specific to Maricopa County, the general structure may be adapted for use in other jurisdictions facing similar heat-related health threats.

History and Rationale 
 
Maricopa County, home to more than 4.5 million residents,1 is the fourth-largest county in the 
United States by population. The region’s extreme summer temperatures place both residents and 
visitors at elevated risk for heat-related illness and death. 
MCDPH launched its heat surveillance program in 2006 in collaboration with the Maricopa County 
Office of the Medical Examiner (OME), following a 2005 heatwave that claimed 45 lives, 35 of 
them within nine consecutive days. Since then, the system has expanded to integrate multiple 
data streams and automated processes, providing real-time data that guides public health action. 
The case definition applies year-round, though most surveillance activities occur during the 
annual heat season (May–October). 
Since its inception, the surveillance system has continued to evolve through key milestones. 
Weekly and annual heat reports became publicly available starting in 2007. In 2012, MCDPH 
incorporated air conditioning and energy use data from OME Preliminary Investigative Reports 
(PIRs) to better capture contributing factors in heat-related deaths. In 2024, the system underwent 
full-scale modernization, replacing static reports and manual processes with automated

workflows, a public-facing interactive dashboard, and enhanced data management systems to 
improve efficiency and usability. (See Figure 1 for a visual overview of key milestones.) MCDPH 
methodology supports internal consistency and offers a replicable framework for external 
partners. 
 
 
Heat Surveillance Methodology 
 
Seasonality 
 
The enhanced heat surveillance season typically runs from May through October, coinciding with increased high and average daily temperatures (heat season). During this time, heat-related illnesses and deaths are tracked and monitored daily. Outside this window, public health does not routinely report heat-related illnesses; however, heat-related deaths are monitored year-round. These data are used to inform emergency and non-emergency operations outside the typical season. Historical case data show that deaths have occurred as early as March and as late as November. Key Definitions 
 
• 
Environmental heat: heat generated by natural outdoor conditions (e.g., sun exposure, 
ambient temperature, humidity). Excludes artificial sources (e.g., stoves, industrial 
equipment).  
• 
Heat-caused deaths: Deaths in which environmental heat directly caused the death, 
indicated in Part I (primary cause) of the death certificate (see Figure 3). 
• 
Heat-contributed deaths: Deaths in which environmental heat contributed to the 
sequence of conditions leading to death but was not the direct cause, noted in Part II 
(contributing causes) of the death certificate (see Figure 3). 
• 
Heat season: Period when enhanced heat surveillance is conducted, extreme high 
temperatures are expected, and community heat relief resources are available (May-
October). 
• 
Heat surveillance: Active monitoring of heat-related deaths and illnesses.

Process Summary 
 
The Maricopa County heat surveillance system follows a structured three-phase process: 
1) Extract data and identify/classify cases  
2) Standardize circumstantial variables 
3) Link heat-related illness and heat-related deaths for integrated analysis and reporting.  
 
The following sections detail each component and describe the full process flow from data 
collection to public dissemination. This methodology ensures consistent, timely monitoring 
during the heat season and supports real-time public health response. 
 
Phase 1: Data Sources, Data Extraction, and Potential Heat-
Related Case Identification and Classification 
 
1.1 Data Sources and Potential Case Identification 
Heat-related death and heat-related illness surveillance in Maricopa County is based on data 
obtained from the following sources:  
 
• 
Maricopa County Office of the Medical Examiner (OME): The OME investigates deaths that 
are sudden, unexpected, violent, suspicious, or accidental (see Arizona Revised Statute 11-
5935). As part of these investigations, the OME identifies potential heat-related deaths, which 
MCDPH uses to report cases under investigation. For each case, the OME completes a 
Preliminary Investigative Report (PIR) containing investigative details and conducts a full 
medicolegal death investigation, including toxicology testing for potential substance-related 
deaths. 
 
Prior to 2024, the OME provided MCDPH with potential heat-related deaths in Excel format, 
including demographics, preliminary circumstances, and cause of death details. Beginning in 
2024, MCDPH gained access to an internal OME Power BI dashboard, which contains the 
same information and allows direct data download by MCDPH heat surveillance staff.  
 
• 
MCDPH and Arizona Department of Health Services (ADHS) Office of Vital Registration 
(OVR): All Maricopa County death certificates are registered in the Arizona Department of 
Health Services electronic death registration system (EDRS), also known as Database 
Application for Vital Events (DAVE), which is shared across counties. OVR provides MCDPH’s

Division of Epidemiology and Informatics with daily extracts for all decedents who were 
Maricopa County residents or who died in the county.  
 
To identify additional potential heat-related deaths not investigated by the OME, MCDPH 
surveillance staff run daily queries of EDRS records. These searches scan Parts I and II of 
death certificates using key terms (e.g., heat stroke, hyperthermia) and relevant ICD-10 codes 
(e.g., X30, T67.X).

deaths and extreme heat events using internal communications tracking systems maintained 
by the MCDPH Communications Team. Relevant media-reported information is manually 
entered into the open-text notes field within the heat surveillance system using the PIR data 
entry form (Phase 3).

1.2 Case Compilation and Database Development 
Data from the sources described in Section 1.1 are merged to create the MCDPH Heat-Related 
Death Database (Figure 3), housed on a secure server managed by the MCDPH Division of 
Epidemiology and Informatics.  
 
To be included, deaths must either:  
1) Have an activated, OME heat attribute (criteria for being listed on the OME Power BI 
dashboard), or 
2) Have a primary or contributing cause of death in the Database Application for Vital 
Events (DAVE), Arizona’s electronic death registration system (EDRS), that includes 
one of the terms in Figure 2.  
 
The SAS program uses identifying variables to compile records and reduce duplication. Records 
are primarily merged using the Medical Examiner case number, death certificate number or a 
combination of full name and date of birth. The database is updated daily during the enhanced 
heat surveillance season and maintained by the Division of Epidemiology and Informatics. A data 
dictionary outlining key variable definitions is included in Appendix A.  
 
As OME or OVR records are updated, new information overwrites older data automatically, and 
new information is saved over the older information during the merging process; updates 
therefore occur automatically. See the Quality Assurance section for additional details.

1.3 Heat-Related Death Case Classification 
Classifying a case from under-investigation to a confirmed or ruled out case may take 90 days or 
longer. To ensure consistency, all heat-related deaths are categorized using standardized MCDPH 
definitions (Figure 4). Both heat-caused and heat-contributed deaths are tracked. For example, a 
death with hyperthermia due to environmental heat exposure listed in Part I is classified as heat-
caused, while a death listing heat exposure in Part II alongside a drug overdose is classified as

heat-contributed. Other jurisdictions may report only heat-caused deaths, which should be noted 
when comparing methods. This guides how each case is reviewed and classified by MCDPH.

Case classification occurs after a cause of death is determined. As causes of death are submitted 
to vital registration, the SAS program described in section 1.2 automatically extracts relevant Part 
I and Part II information and classifies the case as heat-caused or heat-contributed.  
 
Under-investigation cases with a completed cause of death that do not meet criteria for heat-
caused or heat-contributed are initially ruled out and excluded from analyses. Before final 
exclusion, each cause of death is manually reviewed to ensure correct classification.  
 
Phase 2: Standardize Circumstantial Data 
 
2.1 Preliminary Investigative Report (PIR) Request  
Once a death is confirmed as heat-related by the OME, MCDPH staff access the OME Preliminary 
Investigative Report (PIR) via an online 
 application to extract relevant data. PIRs provide details beyond the death certificate, capturing 
environmental, behavioral, and situational risk factors at the time of death. 
 
Circumstantial details extracted include housing status, air conditioning availability and 
functionality, location of death (indoor vs. outdoors), substance use indicators, medical history 
and comorbidities, and activity at the time of injury (e.g., hiking, biking, working). A list of variables 
extracted from the PIR for heat surveillance purposes is included in Appendix B. Data from PIRs 
are integrated into the MCDPH Heat-Related Death Database in Phase 3. 
 
2.2 Standardizing PIR Data Entry Using SharePoint 
Previously, PIR data were entered manually into Excel spreadsheets, often as free text, making 
standardization and data cleaning time-consuming and error prone. The current process uses a 
 
Maricopa County • Department of Public Health • Epidemiology & Informatics • January 2026       9 
SharePoint Entry Form with multiple-choice fields for each variable of interest (see Appendix C), 
ensuring consistent data and reducing variability from free-text entries. 
  
Aside from saving OME data to MCDPH servers, this is the only manual data entry step in the 
surveillance workflow; all other steps, including exporting the heat death database, merging data 
sources, and case classification, are automated. The SharePoint form automatically exports 
responses to Excel, which are then imported into SAS and merged with heat-related death records 
(Figure 5).

additional documentation is obtained. This eliminates introducing assumptions during the initial data capture. 
 
2.3 Circumstantial Variable Classification 
As previously mentioned, MCDPH does not confirm the cause of death or determine whether a 
death is heat-related. MCDPH staff extract only relevant information from the PIRs for 
surveillance purposes.  
 
Additional classifications: 
 
Substance-involved heat-related deaths 
 
Maricopa County • Department of Public Health • Epidemiology & Informatics • January 2026       10 
MCDPH uses cause-of-death fields and toxicology data to identify cases involving drugs or 
alcohol, which are defined as those where the death certificate includes an ICD-10 code for drug 
overdose (X40–X44, X60–X64, X85, or Y10–Y14) or acute alcohol poisoning (X45, X65, or Y15). 
MCDPH uses these codes in combination with OME toxicology data and PIR information to 
monitor patterns of drug- or alcohol-involved heat-related deaths. However, not all drugs detected 
in toxicology necessarily played a causal or contributory role in the deaths; some deaths may not 
have complete toxicology results, and OME determines whether substance use contributed to 
death.  
 
Indoor deaths are further classified according to the cooling environment. If an air conditioning 
(A/C) unit was present but inoperable, broken, or could not be turned on despite the presence of 
electricity, the case is categorized as nonfunctioning. If the A/C unit was working but intentionally 
turned off for some reason at the time of the OME scene inspection, it is marked as functioning 
but off. If the A/C unit could not operate due to a power shutoff, regardless of whether it was 
functioning or nonfunctioning, the case is designated as having no electricity.  
 
Homelessness is determined using the listed address and corroborating information in PIRs and 
case notes. If no fixed address is listed, or the location is known to be a shelter, government 
agency, business, or intersection, the decedent is classified as experiencing homelessness. 
Cases are also classified as experiencing homelessness if there is an indication on the death 
certificate. If the address is listed as out of jurisdiction, then time spent in Arizona, as provided 
by the death certificate, is taken into consideration.  
 
 
Phase 3: Link, Analyze, and Report Heat-Related Illnesses and 
Heat-Related Deaths 
 
3.1 Identify and Classify Heat-Related Illness (HRI) Cases  
During the heat season (May–October), MCDPH conducts syndromic surveillance for heat-related 
illness using NSSP ESSENCE data and the Heat-Related Illness v2 case definition.8 
 
As part of the modernization efforts, MCDPH developed an automated R program that retrieves 
HRI cases, demographic variables of HRI cases, and temperature metrics directly from ESSENCE 
and integrates them into the surveillance workflow. The dataset has also been expanded to 
capture contextual factors, including whether the visits involved occupational exposure, 
recreational activity, or substance use.  
 
 
3.2 Additional Heat-Related Illness Surveillance  
In addition to near real-time syndromic surveillance, MCDPH periodically produces annual and 
multi-year HRI reports using hospital discharge data. For these reports, cases are identified using 
the National Environmental Public Health Tracking Network heat-related illness definition.9  
 
Maricopa County • Department of Public Health • Epidemiology & Informatics • January 2026       11 
 
3.3 Merge Heat-Related Illness Data with Heat-Related Death Data  
After heat-related illness data are extracted from ESSENCE, daily summary counts are then linked 
to heat-related deaths data by date in the Heat Surveillance Data Table and are tabulated for 
summary and visualization purposes as described in section 3.3 (Figure 6). 
 
Deaths are counted by date of death, and illnesses are counted by date of illness or hospital visit; 
individuals may appear in both datasets if there is evidence of heat illness before their death.  
 
This merged dataset supports the weekly updating of MCDPH’s public-facing Power BI 
dashboard, used by internal staff and the general public.   
 
Figure 6. Heat-Related Illness (Morbidity) and Heat-Related Death (Mortality) Integration 
Workflow. 
  
3.4 Stratification and Analysis of Heat-Related Illness and Death Data 
 
After merging, the records are then stratified by key demographic variables, including age group, 
sex, and race/ethnicity. MCDPH typically presents counts instead of rates for surveillance reports, 
because heat-related deaths and illnesses definitions include anyone who visited a Maricopa 
County hospital or died in Maricopa County, regardless of residency status.  
However, when comparing across populations or jurisdictions and over time, MCDPH calculates 
heat-related illness or heat-related death rates using the formula:  
 
 
Maricopa County • Department of Public Health • Epidemiology & Informatics • January 2026       12 
Rate = (Number of HRI cases ÷ Population) x 100,000. 
 
Rates are calculated only for annual reports and are limited to Maricopa County residents. 
Population denominators are sourced from the American Community Survey (ACS) 5-year 
estimates, table DP05.10 Rates are stratified by sex, age groups, race and ethnicity, and housing 
status, such as whether the individual was experiencing homelessness. Additional stratifications 
include the type of hospital visit, whether it was an emergency department visit or inpatient 
admission, the length of the hospital stay, and the activity involved at the time of illness, such as 
working outdoors, walking, or resting indoors without access to cooling.  
 
The data are analyzed for both real-time surveillance and for MCDPH’s more detailed annual or 
multi-year heat-related illness reports, which offer expanded analyses of emergency department 
and inpatient data. These reports help contextualize seasonal trends and assess risk profiles by 
exploring patient demographics, comorbidities (such as chronic illness or substance use), 
housing conditions, illness location, and exposure-related behaviors (e.g., occupational or 
recreational activity, access to air conditioning, or recent substance use).  
 
3.5 Special Considerations for Using the Heat-Related Death Database 
The following considerations are applied when managing and reporting data from the Heat-
Related Deaths Database to ensure accuracy, consistency and appropriate interpretation: 
 
• 
Case inclusion: The dataset includes all heat deaths that occurred in Maricopa County, 
regardless of decedents’ residency. 
o When calculating rates, use resident deaths only as the numerator, to align with 
the census data in the rate denominators. 
• 
Risk factor and demographic analyses: Should be filtered to confirmed cases, 
excluding those under investigation or ruled out. 
• 
Geographic analyses: Always use the location of injury (where a decedent was found 
deceased, or found prior to being transported by Emergency Services and later passed 
away in a healthcare facility).  
• 
Temporal consistency: Data inclusion protocols have evolved over time. Before 
reporting out, verify whether variables were available for the entire year under 
consideration. 
• 
Small numbers: Exclude data with counts less than 5.

Quality Assurance 
 
Ensuring the accuracy and consistency of surveillance data is a central component of MCDPH’s 
methodology. As part of its modernization efforts, MCDPH implemented an automated quality 
control program within SAS that flags discrepancies in case classification, coding 
inconsistencies, and updates to existing records. For example, when a case status changes from 
under investigation to confirmed heat-related deaths, the system generates a prompt for review 
to ensure the change aligns with established definitions and supporting evidence. (See Figure 8 
for an overview of the Quality Control Program.) 
 
Maricopa County • Department of Public Health • Epidemiology & Informatics • January 2026       14 
 
All flagged records are reviewed by a trained epidemiologist who compares PIR data, death 
certificate language, and the Medical Examiner’s annual report. This review verifies that case 
classifications are supported by available data and consistent with internal guidelines. If 
inconsistencies or errors are detected, records are manually corrected by MCDPH staff in the 
heat-related death database and SAS programs before inclusion in dashboards or public 
reporting. 
 
Figure 8. Overview of Quality Control Program. 
 
MCDPH also maintains a case log of all cases reported by OME on a yearly basis, regardless of 
whether the case was eventually ruled out as a heat-related death, in order to track updates over 
time. Cases in the existing Heat Deaths Database for the current year are compiled regardless of 
whether they eventually become “confirmed” or “ruled out”. This enables retrospective audits and 
long-term trend validation. This process supports both real-time surveillance reliability and 
continuous system improvement, as it helps identify recurring issues, refine automated search 
terms, and strengthen data entry protocols. 
 
Although automation has significantly reduced manual workload, quality assurance remains a 
hybrid process, combining rule-based system checks with human expertise. This blended model 
ensures high data fidelity while allowing the system to adapt to emerging conditions, changes in 
reporting standards, or newly-identified risk patterns.

Limitations 
 
This surveillance methodology is subject to several limitations that may affect the completeness, 
accuracy, and timeliness of reported data. First, the system relies heavily on the quality and 
availability of data from the Maricopa County Office of the Medical Examiner (OME) and the 
MCDPH Office of Vital Registration. These primary data streams may occasionally contain 
missing or delayed information, particularly during periods of high caseloads or overlapping 
emergencies. For all OME data, it can take up to 90 days for most cases to be fully confirmed and 
for all surveillance variables to become available.  
 
The availability of Preliminary Investigative Reports (PIRs) is another constraint. While PIRs offer 
valuable circumstantial details and are readily accessible, they may not contain all of the 
information needed at the time of initial case identification. This delay can impact the ability to 
 
Maricopa County • Department of Public Health • Epidemiology & Informatics • January 2026       16 
fully classify deaths or understand contextual factors such as air conditioning status or housing 
type until several weeks after initial identification. 
 
Although MCDPH has automated many parts of the data workflow, manual review remains 
essential for cases that are ambiguous or contain conflicting information. These cases require 
an epidemiologist's review, and as a result, classification decisions may be delayed or revised as 
more information becomes available. 
 
Population estimates used for rate calculations, particularly those related to people experiencing 
homelessness, also present challenges. These estimates are derived from American Community 
Survey (ACS) 5-year estimates, Table DP05. To learn more about how to use ACS data, visit the 
U.S. Census Bureau’s Guidance for Data Users.11 A reference table of population denominator 
sources by subgroup is provided in Appendix D to support transparency and replicability of rate 
calculations. 
 
In addition, while syndromic surveillance (NSSP ESSENCE) offers near real-time data for hospital-
based illness, it does not capture heat-related illness among individuals who do not seek medical 
care within a hospital or emergency department. This may lead to underreporting of less severe 
cases or those treated outside of traditional healthcare settings. 
 
Finally, there may be jurisdictional variability in how heat-related illness and death are 
documented, classified, and reported, which can limit the comparability of Maricopa County’s 
surveillance data with those from other regions. 
 
Anticipated Changes for 2026

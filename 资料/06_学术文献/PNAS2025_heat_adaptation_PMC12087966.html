Significance

We evaluate how three cities in different climates (Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami) can best adapt to increasingly prevalent and intense heatwaves. We assess the effects of street trees, cool roofs, green roofs, rooftop solar panels, and reflective pavements on outdoor heat stress, air conditioning (AC) energy use, and ventilation of air pollution during contemporary and future heatwaves. Street trees shade pedestrians, reducing heat stress four times more effectively than any other strategy. Rooftop solar panels with energy storage can generate enough power for AC systems. Cool and green roofs moderately improve outcomes, unlike cool pavements. Where air quality effects of street-level emissions are of less concern, the combination of street trees and rooftop solar panels is optimal across the cities studied.

Keywords: extreme heat events, urban heat mitigation, outdoor thermal comfort, air pollutant dispersion, space cooling energy use

Abstract

Globally, cities face increasing extreme heat, impacting comfort, health, and energy consumption. Infrastructure-based heat adaptation strategies can improve these outcomes, but each strategy has a unique mix of benefits and drawbacks. Here, we apply an urbanized meteorological model (WRF) with the newly integrated multilayer BEP-Tree street tree model to dynamically downscale Earth System Model projections and a 3-D microclimate model (TUF-Pedestrian) to simulate the street-scale radiation environment impacting pedestrians. We evaluate the performance of five heat adaptation strategies (street trees, cool roofs, green roofs, rooftop photovoltaics (PV), and reflective pavements) during extreme heat events in three cities with contrasting background climates (Toronto, Phoenix, and Miami), under contemporary and end-of-century projected climates, based on three metrics: outdoor heat stress, air conditioning (AC) energy use, and ventilation of vehicular air pollution. No single adaptation strategy improves all three outcomes. While street trees inhibit ventilation, they reduce outdoor heat stress four times more effectively than the next best strategy via shade provision, fully offsetting heat stress increases under a high-emissions end-of-century climate scenario in all cities studied. Cool roofs and green roofs moderately reduce heat stress and energy use. Alternatively, rooftop PV with energy storage can generate sufficient power for space cooling but have marginal effects on heat stress. Reflective pavements are the least effective across metrics. Where the ventilation of street-level emissions is of less concern, our results clearly support the combination of street trees and rooftop PV as a highly complementary and effective means of adaptive mitigation across different climates and neighborhood densities.

The impacts of urbanization and climate change are expected to intensify through the century, leading to increases in the duration, frequency, and intensity of extreme heat events and exacerbation of heat hazards in cities (1–3). This motivates cities to explore infrastructure-based strategies to adapt to elevated temperatures. Such strategies include street trees, cool and green roofs, rooftop photovoltaics (PV), and reflective pavements, among others. Additionally, the US Inflation Reduction Act has allocated substantial funds for urban green infrastructure projects to promote heat management, flood management, and emissions reductions (4). These infrastructures must be carefully evaluated in terms of their ability to improve practical outcomes in cities, both for contemporary and future climates.

Urban heat adaptation measures can be assessed against several outcomes (5), like 2 m air temperature (e.g., 6–8) or building energy use (e.g., 9–11). Many adaptation strategies, like street trees, aim to reduce pedestrian heat stress through not only reductions in air temperature but also, for example, by providing shade (12), which varies at much finer scales compared to air temperature and humidity. Due to the multiscale nature of these components of urban heat stress, few studies consider all the important processes and scales (i.e., 100–105 m). Furthermore, the effects of urban trees on pedestrian heat stress in future climates have only been studied using empirical-statistical downscaling (13, 14), which employs relations that may no longer be valid under future climates (15).

Another important outcome that is influenced by heat adaptation infrastructure is air quality, via effects on deposition, ventilation, and mixing (16–18). During extreme heat, urban air pollution levels typically increase (19, 20), and Filleul et al. (21) found that excess deaths during a heatwave increased by 10% in a selection of French cities due to increased pollution alone. Therefore, it is imperative to assess the effects of heat adaptation strategies on the ventilation of air pollution during heatwaves. However, studies on this topic are lacking for heatwaves during projected future climates. Studies of the impacts of urban heat adaptation strategies on air quality yield mixed results but generally indicate that they degrade air quality (17, 18, 22, 23), mostly due to lower boundary-layer heights and decreased mixing (24).

No single heat adaptation strategy appears to be beneficial in all respects. For example, studies show that cool roofs are effective at reducing air temperature and air conditioning (AC) energy use (7, 25), but they do not provide shade for pedestrians; street trees can strongly reduce outdoor heat stress (14, 26–28) but often increase street-level air pollution through inhibition of mixing (22) and emission of organic aerosols (29); reflective pavements may lower air temperature (30) but increase reflected sunlight on buildings (9) and pedestrians (31); and PV roofs generate electricity that could potentially power AC systems and reduce greenhouse gas emissions but, despite early modeling studies predicting cooling (32, 33), appear to have equivocal effects on air temperature when applied on conventional roofs (34). Similarly, Oke (35) and Martilli (36) showed that different urban forms and vegetation levels could have trade-offs between thermal comfort, air quality, and energy use. However, no studies have compared the performance of a range of adaptation strategies across these three outcomes. Additionally, the efficacy of urban heat adaptation strategies is modulated by the background climate (37–39). Therefore, distinct urban configurations in different background climates may respond differently to each heat adaptation strategy, indicating a need to study adaptation strategies in a range of neighborhoods and climates. However, heat adaptation strategies have scarcely been evaluated with physically based modeling for extreme heat during projected future scenarios and never multiple strategies for cities in multiple climate zones.

In this study, we use a dynamical downscaling approach to investigate multiple outcomes and their trade-offs arising from implementation of urban heat adaptation infrastructure, and the physical processes driving their different effects in diverse background climates. We assess heat adaptation strategies in three contrasting North American cities during contemporary and projected end-of-century (EoC) heatwaves for two climate change and urban development pathways. More specifically, our central research question is: How do street trees, rooftop PV, cool roofs, green roofs, and reflective pavements perform during start-of-century (SoC) and EoC heatwaves in Phoenix (Koppen climate group B: hot, arid), Toronto (Koppen climate group D: continental), and Miami (Koppen climate group A: tropical, humid), in terms of pedestrian thermal stress, AC energy use, and dispersion of pollutants in the urban canopy layer?

Each city and time period combination is simulated for a “no adaptation” base case using a dynamical downscaling methodology (40) and resimulated with the city-wide addition of each adaptation strategy to quantify the corresponding impact. We evaluate pedestrian heat stress, building AC energy use for space cooling, and dispersion of tailpipe emissions during representative heatwaves at the SoC (1999–2018), as well as for EoC (2081-2100) under two emissions scenarios (Representative Concentration Pathways, or RCPs, 4.5 and 8.5 (41)). The core of this methodology applies the Weather Research and Forecasting (WRF) mesoscale meteorological model with a state-of-the-art, multilayer urban canopy and building energy model BEP-BEM (42–44) to dynamically downscale outdoor climate, building energy use, and pollutant dispersion for contemporary and projected future extreme heat events. Subsequently, output from WRF drives the microscale TUF-Pedestrian model (45) for assessment of pedestrian exposure to terrestrial and solar radiation (e.g., shade) at sidewalk locations in urban canyons (Section 4.4). To quantify outdoor heat stress we employ the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI), an “equivalent temperature” index which captures the physiological impacts of solar and terrestrial radiation, as quantified by the mean radiant temperature [TMRT (46)], in addition to humidity, wind speed, and air temperature. Adaptation strategies are simulated with both WRF and TUF-Pedestrian, and simulations represent a maximum potential application intensity of each strategy to bracket their possible effects. We integrate the BEP-Tree multilayer street tree model (47) within WRF for the first time in order to simulate the effects of street trees on our outcomes of interest with a well-evaluated, physically based model. In the following sections, we present the impacts of all five heat adaptation strategies on outdoor pedestrian heat stress during extreme heat, followed by impacts on space cooling energy use, and finally, impacts on air pollutant dispersion. Based on these results, we present a prioritization of the five heat adaptation strategies assessed here. A summary of the methodology applied, including the developments to the Jiang et al. (40) methodology, is presented in Section 4, and Fig. 1 provides an overall summary graphic.

1. Results

1.1. Outdoor Heat Stress.

Heat stress is expected to increase by the EoC for all cities and emissions scenarios (i.e., Representative Concentration Pathways, or RCPs, 4.5 or 8.5) examined, absent adaptation strategies (“Base case” in Fig. 2). For example, the average daytime UTCI (7 AM to 7 PM) in Phoenix during the hottest week of the year is expected to increase from 43.3 °C to 45.6 °C under RCP 4.5, and reach 46.2 °C under RCP 8.5 by the EoC. Corresponding UTCI increases of 2.7 (6.2) °C are expected for Toronto, and of 2.2 (3.8) °C for Miami, under RCP 4.5 (8.5), on top of contemporary UTCI values of 32.9 °C and 35.8 °C, respectively. These warming magnitudes during extreme heat are similar to the mean projected summertime increase in air temperature for these cities.

Most adaptation strategies considered here reduce heat stress to some extent (Fig. 2). However, street trees strongly outperform other strategies. They are highly effective at reducing UTCI in both contemporary and future urban climates via provision of shade, and to a lesser extent, via transpirational cooling, reducing daytime UTCI averaged across all three time periods and all sidewalk locations (sunlit and shaded by buildings) by 4.0 °C, 4.3 °C, and 2.9 °C in Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami, respectively (Fig. 2). They are especially effective at reducing pedestrian UTCI during the hottest times of day—especially in the hot, arid climate of Phoenix, where they are able to reduce the hours of extreme heat stress experienced by pedestrians to below contemporary levels even under the RCP 8.5 scenario (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Street tree cooling and associated mechanisms are further discussed in Section 2.3.

No other heat adaptation strategy examined here is able to lower heat stress at the EoC to contemporary levels under either RCP. The next best strategy is cool roofs, which reduce daytime UTCI in Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami by an average of 1.1 °C, 1.5 °C, and 0.4 °C, respectively, relative to the base case (Fig. 2). Cool roofs are most effective during the day and have a residual effect at night (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

Green roofs and PV roofs are comparatively less effective at reducing pedestrian heat stress. Rooftop PVs tend to have negligible effects during the day but cool off rapidly at night due to their small heat capacity, modestly decreasing air temperature and yielding a diurnal average UTCI reduction of 0.2, 0.7, and 0.0 °C in Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). Green roofs tend to decrease daytime UTCI by an average of 0.5 °C, 0.5 °C, and 0.1 °C in Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami, respectively (Fig. 2). We note that the current roof scenario is limited to 25 mm/wk of irrigation and may reduce UTCI further if they are better irrigated, especially in arid Phoenix. However, most currently installed green roofs are not irrigated and may not perform as well during daytime extreme heat.

Finally, we find that reflective pavements have an equivocal effect on heat stress. They increase urban albedo, thereby cooling air and ground surface temperatures. However, applied in the configuration tested here (covering the entire street and sidewalk), they also tend to reflect sunlight toward pedestrians during daylight hours, increasing their radiant load. For that reason, they are not as effective as cool roofs in Phoenix (0.4 °C reduction in UTCI), and they have a negligible effect on UTCI in Toronto (0.0 °C) and Miami (0.2 °C reduction). They are slightly more effective in higher-density neighborhoods where pedestrians are more often in shade, but the difference is less than 0.1 °C of UTCI (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Reflective pavements are further discussed in Section 2.4.

Street trees can be combined with rooftop mitigation strategies for even greater heat stress reduction. Combining the air temperature cooling of cool roofs with the shading of street trees leads to the maximum reduction in heat stress in all cases that we examined (Fig. 2). Taking hours of strong heat stress as the metric, this combination of strategies is sufficiently effective to reduce heat stress during heatwaves to contemporary levels in all cities studied, even under RCP 8.5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9).

The heat stress results presented above assume a built-up environment with a high density of buildings consistent with a “compact midrise” or Local Climate Zone 2 [LCZ 2 (48)] area. However, we find broadly similar results for less dense “open lowrise” areas (LCZ 6) common in these North American cities, with the exception that baseline heat stress increases in all cases due to decreased shading during the hottest times of day (SI Appendix, Fig. S10).

1.2. AC Energy Use.

AC energy use for space cooling, as modeled by WRF-BEP-BEM in all three cities and time period-emissions scenario combinations (Section 4.1), is expected to increase during extreme heat by the EoC as a result of urban development and associated population increases, and climate change, both as a daily average (Fig. 3) and during peak daily demand (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). In Phoenix, for example, diurnal average AC use is projected to increase by 65% by the EoC under RCP 4.5 and more than double under RCP 8.5. Most of this increase is due to urban development—which we consider for Phoenix and Miami but are unable to consider for Toronto (Section 4.2)—rather than climate change, which is considered for all three cities (40). However, we find that, even absent urban development and associated population increases or increases in AC penetration, Toronto’s AC use during heatwaves is projected to increase by 61% under an RCP 8.5 scenario.

Cool roofs can mitigate part of these increases. When we only consider climate change effects, they can reduce AC energy use in Toronto to below contemporary levels under RCP 4.5 (Fig. 3), but not under RCP 8.5, nor under either future scenario in Phoenix or Miami with urban development accounted for. Green roof impacts are similar to those of cool roofs, and outperform them when considering peak AC energy use (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), in large part because peak AC energy use occurs later in the day when downwelling solar radiation, which cool roofs more directly act on, is lower.

Full coverage of all rooftops with PV not only reduces AC energy use by sheltering the roof from solar radiation, it also generates enough power on a daily timescale to completely power AC units in all scenarios examined, assuming a 30% conversion efficiency (Fig. 3). Assuming this efficiency, a citywide PV roof coverage of 89%, 57%, and 81% would be sufficient in Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami to cover AC energy use under RCP 8.5, assuming no change in the AC penetration rate. Excess energy would be available for export to the grid. However, for a more typical contemporary efficiency of 15%, AC energy use could not be offset for any time period in Phoenix, at EoC for either RCP in Miami, nor for EoC RCP 8.5 in Toronto. Moreover, this power generation peaks around solar noon, whereas peak space cooling demand is reached in the late afternoon, at which time rooftop PV does not offset AC energy use (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Therefore, the lag between peak rooftop PV electricity generation and peak demand for AC-related cooling, as well as the inability to generate power at night, limit the ability for this strategy to mitigate concurrent blackout and extreme heat compound events unless paired with sufficient electricity or thermal storage.

The cooling and shading effects of street trees are more able to reduce cooling need during such compound events, at least in Phoenix and Toronto, reducing thermal load on buildings (Fig. 3) as well as making outdoor spaces more thermally comfortable (Fig. 2). Furthermore, the large street tree increases we simulate have the potential to decrease electricity use from AC in Phoenix by 35 to 43% and in Toronto by 25 to 45%, including at peak times (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Benefits are smaller in Miami, with a reduction of 10 to 17%, due to the limitation of tree transpiration in humid climates. We note that in all cases, the presence of the 10 m tall street trees assumed here reduces the amount of power generated from PVs at certain times of day due to shading of PV panels on buildings that are shorter, i.e. 5 m tall (Section 2.3.1).

We showed that street trees can reduce environmental heating of buildings; we also found that rooftop PV operating at 30% efficiency is able to generate sufficient power not only to compensate for climate change and urban development but also to completely power all AC use during a heatwave, both on a city-wide scale and for most individual neighborhoods. Thus, the combination of street trees and rooftop PVs with adequate electricity (or thermal) storage provides the best heat adaptation from a power production/energy use standpoint, and it can help mitigate concurrent blackout and extreme heat events (49, 50) if accessible to those most at risk.

1.3. Air Pollutant Dispersion.

While our results indicate that street trees are effective at reducing AC loading and especially pedestrian heat stress, they can present a significant drawback in terms of urban ventilation. Greater residence time (i.e., concentration divided by emission rate) of ground-level tailpipe emissions, simulated here as passive tracers (Section 4.2) emitted in the lowest WRF model layer (0 to 4 m), corresponds to lower street-level ventilation (Fig. 4). The large effect of trees on urban ventilation can be explained not only by the decrease in turbulent mixing in the boundary-layer air immediately above the city due to the surface-level cooling they provide but also by the drag they exert, reducing wind speed and therefore street-level ventilation (also see Section 2.1 for a discussion of tree processes not represented in this study). Our simulations suggest that rooftop PV panels may be effective at increasing ventilation in Phoenix when street trees are present, perhaps due to the high amounts of sensible heat they shed into the boundary-layer during daytime in such a hot environment. This effect is not as consistent in the other cities studied, so further investigation is warranted.

Cool roofs also worsen ventilation, likely by cooling the urban surface and reducing sensible heat flux, which decreases planetary boundary layer depth over which tracers are mixed. When combined with street trees, they compound the poorer ventilation, further increasing tracer residence time for most cases simulated (Fig. 4). Overall, all adaptation strategies reduce ventilation. However, cool roofs, and especially street trees, do so to the greatest extent.

1.4. Prioritizing Heat Adaptation Strategies.

In the preceding sections, we showed that each heat adaptation strategy or combination of strategies has a different effect on each of the three outcomes investigated in this study. Fig. 5 illustrates the effects of all heat adaptation strategies and select combinations thereof on daily integrated outdoor heat stress (quantified as the average daily sum of degree-hours above a UTCI threshold of 26 °C, the boundary between “no heat stress” and “slight heat stress,” averaged over the four sidewalk locations simulated—see Section 4.4), and on daily average AC energy use for the entirety of each metropolitan area. Most adaptation strategies we simulate reduce heat stress to some extent. However, street trees strongly outperform the other strategies. When averaged across the three cities and time periods, the magnitude of daytime heat stress reduction associated with street tree expansion was almost four times greater than cool roofs, ten times greater than green roofs, and nineteen times greater than cool paving (Fig. 2). Street trees are highly effective at reducing UTCI in both contemporary and future urban climates via provision of shade and, to a lesser extent, via transpirational cooling. They are especially effective at reducing pedestrian UTCI during the hottest times of day—especially in the hot, arid climate of Phoenix, where, provided their health is guaranteed, they are able to reduce the hours of extreme heat stress experienced by pedestrians to below contemporary levels even under the worst-case RCP 8.5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). We further analyze street tree cooling and associated mechanisms in Section 2.3.

Rooftop PV outperforms the other strategies in terms of AC energy use under the assumption that the energy produced is used to power buildings, even at the lower (typical contemporary) conversion efficiency of 15%, yet this strategy is not effective at reducing outdoor heat stress (Fig. 5). However, rooftop PV can readily be combined with street trees, which strongly outperform all other strategies in terms of outdoor heat stress reduction. Thus, the combination of PV roofs and street trees may yield an optimal result in the cities we study, provided trees do not overly shade PV installations on roofs (Fig. 5). Although the combination of cool roofs and street trees provides even greater outdoor heat stress reduction, this combination is substantially less effective in terms of net AC energy use. In streets with high traffic volumes and greater need for ventilation, street trees may not be feasible, and alternative street-level heat stress reduction tactics may still provide some relief, such as pavement watering, pervious pavement, or ground-level vegetation (none of which are evaluated here). However, with an ongoing transition to electric vehicles, the impacts of reduced ventilation on air quality may be offset by lowered emissions from tailpipes and other sources.

Green roofs appear to be less effective than cool roofs at reducing heat stress but more effective at reducing AC energy use (Fig. 5). These conclusions depend on the particular characteristics of the cool roofs and green roofs as well as the underlying roof substrate (e.g., degree of insulation). Our results suggest that reflective pavements have little effect on heat stress and increase AC energy use in all cases we examine and are therefore not recommended in locations near buildings or pedestrian paths.

Ultimately, the optimal combination of adaptation strategies in a particular street, neighborhood or city will depend on the relative importance of outdoor heat stress mitigation, AC energy use reduction, and street-level ventilation, as well as other potential outcomes linked to urban thermal performance (e.g., winter and shoulder season effects) or not (e.g., stormwater management effects). However, while there are differences between each city (and associated background climate) studied here in terms of the magnitude of the effects of each adaptation strategy, the general findings related to optimal adaptation strategy choice are somewhat insensitive to both background climate and urban density (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Fig. S10). We discuss this further in Section 2.5.

2. Discussion

2.1. Boundary-Layer Height and Pollutant Concentration.

Both observational and modeling studies have linked planetary boundary-layer (PBL) height and air quality (51–53), noting that increased PBL height dilutes pollutants over a greater depth, thus improving air quality. Our WRF results during the afternoon before sunset indicate this relation generally holds (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). However, the correspondence is not perfect. For example, while the PBL height in Phoenix is much deeper than Toronto’s, its surrounding topography may trap pollution more than in Toronto, which is located beside a large lake and whose surroundings are mostly flat.

Even within the same city, the effect of an adaptation strategy on ventilation is not explained solely by its impact on PBL height. Street trees, for example, inhibit ventilation and mixing by reducing street-level wind speed. Trees can also decouple the subcanopy air from the air above, reducing vertical mixing of pollutants (54–56). During the afternoon, the effect of trees on ventilation appears to be explainable for the most part by their effect on PBL height (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). In the morning, however, trees increase tracer residence time to a greater degree than can be explained by the decrease in PBL height. At night, there appears to be a wide range of tracer residence times while the PBL height remains relatively constant, so urban ventilation is being governed not primarily by PBL height but by other processes such as reduction of wind speed and mixing. The range of tracer residence times for the same PBL height at night is especially wide in the presence of street trees, indicating that tree processes unrelated to PBL height are influencing urban ventilation.

In terms of limitations, this study examines the dispersion of passive air pollutants during heatwaves and assumes ground-level emissions proportional to road area and vehicular traffic intensity. We did not investigate chemical processes or physical processes other than ventilation, such as the additional deposition surfaces that trees provide or secondary aerosols that may be produced from gases emitted from trees. Moreover, actual street-level air quality and associated hazards will critically depend on other factors, such as emission strength and distribution, which are not addressed here. Thus, although this study suggests that street trees could decrease ventilation and inhibit dispersion, there remains uncertainty about the nature and magnitude of their overall air quality effects as well as the future air pollution emissions context in which their effects would be realized.

2.2. Urban Heat Adaptation and Air Pollutant Dispersion.

Urban air quality is a major environmental concern. There is no safe exposure level to particulate matter (57). Cities tend to reduce ventilation by increasing drag, creating convergence within the urban canopy. Additionally, many cities are situated in basins and valleys that, under various synoptic conditions, do not ventilate well. We also found that all urban heat adaptation strategies that we assessed reduced dispersion of pollutants emitted at street-level and worsened ventilation (Section 1.2).

Emissions inventories for many parts of the world show that urban air pollutant emissions are expected to decrease substantially due to technological advances (e.g., electrification of the vehicle stock) and more stringent emissions standards (58). As such, despite the additional air quality hazard that some urban heat adaptation strategies may pose, we expect that total risk for air quality will go down worldwide throughout the century. Therefore, our analysis of air pollutant dispersion in this study should be viewed as an estimate of the potential additional hazard from implementing these adaptation strategies rather than a predicted hazard. Moreover, decreases in ventilation may become increasingly acceptable as cities move to address the growing heat hazard. Importantly, while many emission control technologies exist for air pollution, few such technological options are available for ambient heat management.

Given this context of decreasing urban emissions, as well as the exceptional ability of trees to reduce pedestrian heat stress and AC energy use during heatwaves (Section 2.3), we recommend consideration of appropriately sited street trees as an urban heat adaptation strategy despite their admittedly large impact on urban ventilation—even in cities such as Phoenix situated in geographical regions where ventilation is attenuated. However, in cities in the developing world, ventilation of air pollutants may remain important for some time, with increasing particulate concentrations projected over coming decades (58). Additionally, European cities, which are typically more densely built than the North American cities studied here, may also be more susceptible to decreased urban ventilation from street trees. Therefore, we encourage urban planners to consider street tree planting configurations that encourage mixing while nevertheless shading pedestrians and urban facets. For example, shorter hedge barriers appear to inhibit ventilation less than tall trees while shielding pedestrians from vehicular tailpipe emissions (16), and street trees that are spaced out affect the ventilation less than rows of trees (59). We also encourage study of adaptation strategies that increase urban ventilation in the presence of street trees while providing cobenefits, such as scattered high-rise structures (60) or urban designs that promote sea breezes in coastal cities (61). Finally, given that the cooling effect of trees on UTCI stems primarily from shading of pedestrians, gray infrastructures that provide shade should be further studied.

2.3. Street Trees: Their Implementation and Their Effects on Urban Climate.

Trees cool air temperature by shading urban surfaces and diverting some of the absorbed solar radiation into transpiration. They also enhance daytime pedestrian thermal comfort by providing shade. Meili et al. (62) simulated the effects of different tree processes (radiation, transpiration, and drag) on 2-m air temperature in four different climates and concluded that transpiration effects dominated air temperature impacts during the day, especially in Phoenix.

Here, we conduct a similar analysis but for the impacts of trees on outdoor heat exposure instead of air temperature. Considering both the microscale shading impacts of trees and the local- and city-scale air temperature cooling, we find that most of the daytime reduction of pedestrian heat stress by trees is from reduced terrestrial and solar radiation, quantified here as the reduction of mean radiant temperature (Fig. 6). This reduction is principally due to shade, but also stems in part from reduced terrestrial emission from cooler urban facets now shaded by trees. The cooler air temperatures generated by tree transpiration also play a substantial role. In Phoenix, cooling extends into the night, partly due to the shading of high heat capacity materials (i.e., trees are assumed to be placed above asphalt), partly due to slightly open stomata at night combined with high vapor pressure deficits, and partly due to reduced mixing into a thin nocturnal PBL (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) leading to an even greater cooling effect on ground-level air temperature.

Increased humidity from street tree transpiration has a relatively small impact on outdoor heat stress in the continental humid city of Toronto and the tropical humid city of Miami. In contrast, the increased humidity modestly offsets UTCI reductions in the hot, arid city of Phoenix by 0.5 °C to 1.5 °C, under the assumption that the drought-tolerant species simulated here have access to some degree of soil moisture, e.g. via deep roots that access the water table.

Zolch et al. (28) found that extensive implementation of street trees (citywide tree coverage of 36%) offers a 7 °C decrease in physiologically equivalent temperature (PET) at 3 PM, mostly from shading. Here, we find that street trees decrease UTCI by 3 °C to 5 °C at that time of day for a comparable tree cover. While PET has been found to be slightly more variable than UTCI (63), they both attempt to quantify an “equivalent temperature.”

In this study, we assume a canyon-average leaf area index (LAI) increase of 2.58 and a canyon tree cover increase of 67% (equivalent to a citywide tree cover increase of approximately 40% in our study), which corresponds to tree canopy coverage above all sidewalk and street areas except for intersections in our TUF-Pedestrian simulations. Thus, a substantially lower tree canopy cover would be required to provide equivalent shade to pedestrian routes (e.g., sidewalks) only. Due to the extent of the simulated vegetation coverage, we do not investigate tree placements in depth in this study, although it is of importance (64, 65). However, we note that street trees have a greater effect on daytime UTCI on the north side of east–west streets, particularly in neighborhoods with taller buildings and/or smaller setbacks, because these locations receive less building shade (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). As expected, this difference is less prominent for open low-rise (LCZ 6) areas, in which even the south sides of east–west streets are not substantially shaded during summertime. Therefore, cities may wish to prioritize planting trees in open low-rise areas and, in densely built areas, preferentially on the north sides of streets.

The high level of vegetation simulated in this study is within the realm of possibility for many cities: For example, the city of Melbourne aims to increase its citywide tree cover to 40% (66), and cities like New York and Toronto could potentially increase their tree cover by up to 40% of the city’s area (67), although much of this increase may need to occur on private land. However, there are challenges to the implementation of trees at this intensity. More so than trees in urban parks, street trees are a hazard to infrastructure and buildings, especially during storms. They can be difficult to maintain, can be sensitive to drought and future climate extremes, generate allergy-inducing pollen, and the leaf-off of shade-producing trees takes resources to manage (68). Moreover, evergreen trees can block passive solar heating of buildings in cold and shoulder seasons. Our WRF-BEP-Tree simulations assume that the air temperature and humidity effects of existing trees are accounted for by the vegetated portion of each grid square via the Noah soil-vegetation-atmosphere transfer scheme and that added trees are accounted for via BEP-Tree within the BEP-BEM urban scheme in WRF. Critically, the key radiative (e.g., shading) impacts of street trees on outdoor heat stress reduction are captured with TUF-Pedestrian and respond to the local tree cover immediately above the pedestrian locations; these impacts are therefore largely independent of assumptions made about preexisting tree cover. In other words, our simulations detail the potential benefits to outdoor thermal comfort in street locations currently devoid of trees, and benefits will be reduced in locations with some existing street tree coverage.

We assume typical summertime soil water availability for each city we study. When street trees are irrigated in Phoenix they have a slightly larger reduction in heat stress due to the increased transpiration (Fig. 2). Measurements have found that even when water-restricted, apple trees in France in the summer performed at least 70% as well at reducing UTCI when compared with well-watered trees (69). In other words, tree shade is of primary importance for reducing pedestrian heat stress.

More broadly, it has been demonstrated that there are likely to be restrictions in water availability in precisely those regions where urban greenery such as street trees are most effective in cooling through transpiration (70). However, in arid environments, many drought-tolerant trees have deep roots that reach the water table. Given the large effect street trees have on both AC energy use and pedestrian heat stress, allocating some water to street tree irrigation may be worthwhile for some cities even in the face of water restrictions. Decision-makers will need to weigh the benefits of street tree cooling against scarce water resources.

2.3.1. Street tree—PV roof interactions.

When paired with PV roofs, street trees affect net energy use not only through transpirational cooling of the air and shading of buildings but also through shading of PV panels. In Phoenix, Toronto, and Miami, the interaction amounts to 14, 29%, and 0.4%, respectively, of the sum of the net energy savings due to PV roofs alone and street trees alone (Fig. 5). While this antagonistic interaction effect is non-negligible in Phoenix and Toronto, the two strategies remain highly complementary from the perspective of optimization of both outdoor thermal comfort and space cooling energy use.

Both street trees and PV roofs are adaptive mitigation strategies, with street trees sequestering CO2 (71) and rooftop PV reducing the need for power plants powered by fossil fuels as well as some of the embodied emissions associated with utility-scale PV (72). Implemented together, they can offset projected heat stress and projected AC energy use at the EoC. We therefore suggest that, in most scenarios, the street tree-PV roof combination should be prioritized over the cool roof-street tree combination, which also suffers an antagonistic interaction due to tree shading of cool roofs. Moreover, cool roofs have weaker climate mitigation benefits relative to PV roofs. The cool roof-street tree combination may be suitable for buildings where rooftops are not suited for mounting PV panels. Nonetheless, our findings suggest a highly complementary formula for urban planners charged with addressing both emissions reductions and heat adaptation in municipal climate action plans: The combination of street trees and PV roofs was found to clearly outperform any other pairing of strategies for overall urban climate management.

2.4. Performance of Reflective Pavements.

Some cities, such as Los Angeles and Phoenix, have experimented with high-albedo pavements as an urban heat adaptation strategy (73, 74). In our study, reflective pavements reduce air temperature by about 0.5 °C to 1 °C during the day in most cases but have a negligible diurnal impact on UTCI (SI Appendix, Fig. S8), decreasing it at night but sometimes increasing UTCI at the hottest times of day. We also note that reflective pavements increase AC energy use for all cities and all scenarios (Fig. 3), consistent with previous research (9). While they reduce air temperature, they also reflect solar radiation toward buildings, increasing their radiative energy load. Observations in Phoenix found that the local implementation of reflective pavements yielded a 0.5 °C cooling in air temperature but a 2.4 °C increase in TMRT in the afternoon, given a measured albedo increase of 0.20 on average (75). In our study, we considered both the radiative and air temperature effects of reflective pavements and found that, for an albedo increase of 0.23, reflective pavements decreased 2-m air temperature by 1.8 °C but increased TMRT by 3.1 °C during midday in Phoenix, resulting in little net change in UTCI. This represents a maximum feasible implementation in a city with wide streets (in our model, 39.3 m over most of the city). In Toronto, reflective pavements are even less effective at reducing UTCI (SI Appendix, Fig. S13).

Throughout the literature, studies have warned that reflected solar radiation from cool pavements could stress pedestrians (31, 75, 76) and load buildings (9, 10, 77). Given that reflective pavements generate minimal benefit in terms of pedestrian heat stress, building energy, and urban ventilation during heatwaves in all three cities, our findings do not support their implementation, except possibly paved areas unused by pedestrians during the day and sufficiently distant from buildings.

2.5. Effectiveness of Adaptation Strategies in Different Climates.

The optimal combination and intensity of urban heat adaptation measures will vary somewhat between cities and between neighborhoods within each city. We found that in arid climates, transpiration-based adaptation strategies are more effective, provided that sufficient water for irrigation is available, whereas in temperate climates, they are less effective, and in moist tropical climates, they have little to no effect (Section 1.1). Instead, albedo-based adaptation strategies such as cool roofs may be more effective for cities like Toronto. In climates with high humidity such as Miami, shade appears to be the dominant factor for heat stress reduction during extreme heat events, when wind speeds are typically low. Many areas of North America, excluding the Southwestern United States, are projected to become more humid (78), so shade may become increasingly important, be it provided by buildings, trees, or other gray infrastructure such as awnings. Ideally, shade provision is configured to minimally impede wind at the pedestrian level. In the case of Toronto, heat adaptation strategies are projected to become more effective in EoC climates (Fig. 2) since SoC climates do not reach heat stress thresholds as often (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), while in the other two cities, we study heat stress thresholds are consistently met during contemporary extreme heat episodes, making adaptation strategies effective both at the SoC and the EoC.

Each region and neighborhood may value the diverse outcomes resulting from heat adaptation strategies differently, in part because they each face different challenges. In cities and neighborhoods with poorer air quality where adaptation may need to be particularly sensitive to impacts on ventilation, special consideration may need to be given to the coverage, placement, and characteristics of street trees (Section 1.2), including favoring lower hedge barriers that provide additional deposition surfaces and shield pedestrians from traffic emissions (79). In cities where flooding poses a large risk, strategies that enhance stormwater retention, such as green roofs and ground-level greenery, may be favored. In all cases, implementation of heat adaptation measures should be culturally sensitive, equitable, and engage the local population at the planning stages.

In short, different outcomes need to be examined and weighed for different cities and neighborhoods. This study does not consider all effects and externalities associated with heat adaptation strategies, nor does it study the full diversity of neighborhood and block configurations, urban geographic settings, or background climates, all of which are relevant to the choice of heat adaptation strategies. However, the current work captures three key outcomes of relevance to human well-being, resource use, and/or climate change mitigation in urban areas and prioritizes heat adaptation strategy choices for open low-rise and compact midrise neighborhoods in three North American cities that exhibit different summer climates. Moreover, it provides an impetus to look beyond single metrics such as air temperature or pedestrian heat stress when considering urban heat adaptation strategies.

3. Conclusions

Cities are facing increasing challenges related to overheating, and they must consider several forms of adaptation to remain resilient in the face of increasingly severe extreme heat. Nature-based strategies and other heat adaptation infrastructure such as street trees, rooftop PV, cool and green roofs, and reflective pavements are being considered by many municipalities with different morphologies and situated in different background climates. However, each of these strategies presents several potential benefits and drawbacks which require multiscale assessment, particularly outdoor heat stress. Here, for the first time, we apply physically based, urbanized mesoscale and microscale meteorological models to quantify the impacts of these five adaptation strategies during projected future periods of extreme heat in Miami, Phoenix, and Toronto on three outcomes: outdoor heat stress, building AC energy use, and air pollutant dispersion, providing a consistent framework and set of results to enable geographically dependent prioritization of these strategies. In particular, the effects of street trees on these outcomes are investigated using, for the first time, the WRF-integrated multilayer BEP-Tree street tree model. Our findings show that no single heat adaptation strategy is entirely beneficial; they all have trade-offs, and each neighborhood may benefit from a slightly different mix of heat adaptation strategies.

We find that substantial increases of street tree canopy cover can compensate for climate change- and urban development-induced increases in daytime outdoor heat stress at the end of the 21st century, especially in hot, arid climates like Phoenix, but also in more humid coastal climates like Miami. In essence, substantial increases in street tree cover can more than offset projected end-of-century heatwave air temperature increases resulting from climate change in terms of outdoor heat stress, primarily through shading but also transpiration in some climates. Heat stress increases due to humidification from transpiration are small relative to the heat stress reductions provided by shade increases and air temperature decreases. Our simulations show that reduction in AC energy use resulting from widespread tree planting is a substantial cobenefit. Moreover, street trees are known to improve stormwater retention and overall physical (80, 81) and mental well-being of residents (68), in addition to encouraging the use of outdoor space (82, 83), reducing reliance on air-conditioned spaces. We find a considerable decrease in ventilation of tailpipe pollution due to street trees, which may be less of an issue with the electrification of urban transportation and associated projections of decreasing urban emissions. Consistent with the Sailor et al. (34) review, rooftop PV only marginally cools urban air temperature for the cases assessed here, but coverage of the majority of rooftop area generates more than enough electricity for AC use in all three cities during extreme heat given sufficient electrical or thermal storage capacity.

Alternatively, we show that cool roofs can reduce pedestrian heat stress in cities like Toronto and Phoenix. They are also somewhat effective at reducing AC electricity use. Green roofs can reduce AC electricity use as much as cool roofs (or more, during peak AC energy use, which occurs later in the day) due to their insulating properties, but they are not as effective at reducing air temperature at the irrigation level assumed in this study. While reflective pavements can cool air temperature, they increase pedestrian heat stress in the hottest parts of day and also increase energy use for AC.

Overall, our study suggests that the combination of street trees with rooftop PV most effectively reduces outdoor heat stress and net AC energy use for space cooling, at some cost to street-level ventilation of vehicular air pollutants, across all three cities. Although street trees do shade PV panels somewhat in the current study, thereby decreasing their effectiveness, they complement PV roofs by providing reductions in outdoor heat stress. Additionally, both street trees and PV roofs offer climate change mitigation cobenefits, and their combination is a robust example of adaptive mitigation. Assuming roughly equal importance of the three outcomes assessed here, street trees combined with cool roofs present the second-most optimal combination in these three cities. We show that, in densely built areas, street trees should be preferentially planted on the north side of east–west streets to maximize reductions in heat exposure. While not considered in detail here, street tree planting configuration should be optimized to minimize impacts on pollutant ventilation (16). Green roofs and other greenery present important stormwater management benefits that are not considered here. All heat adaptation strategies are likely to have local climate impacts during cold and shoulder seasons that should additionally be evaluated before implementation. Optimal implementation of heat adaptation strategies will ultimately need to be place-based.

This study examined three outcomes for select infrastructure-based adaptation strategies. Several other outcomes are relevant to residents of cities, and not all cobenefits or externalities were considered in this study. Additionally, social and personal adaptation strategies play a key role in urban heat risk during heatwaves, but are outside of the current scope. A quantitative synthesis of all adaptation strategies considering all known benefits, cobenefits, maintenance costs, and externalities is needed to help cities appropriately adapt to coming changes this century, and benefits should be quantified in terms of their effects on mortality, morbidity, and reductions in productivity, which are beyond the current scope. Nevertheless, our results offer clear support for the combination of street tree planting and rooftop PV implementation across different climates and neighborhood densities, and future work should additionally weigh the performance of these strategies as well as cool and green roofs in different seasons and with respect to additional factors such as costs and stormwater retention.

4. Methods

4.1. City-Scale Modeling of Meteorology, Dispersion, and Space Cooling.

The WRF mesoscale weather/climate model version 4.3.3 is applied to downscale ERA-Interim (84) and CESM (85) data to a resolution of 1 km2. We use the WRF-integrated BEP-BEM multilayer urban canyon model (42, 43) to simulate energy exchanges, space cooling, and local-scale climate. The WRF-BEP-BEM model configuration for Phoenix and Toronto has been evaluated against measured air temperature (40). The corresponding model evaluation for Miami is found in SI Appendix, Supplementary Section 1.

Tracer concentration is simulated in WRF-BEP-BEM with the model described in ref. 86. Here, tracer emissions are assumed to be proportional to impervious (building + street) fraction. The emissions flux follows a diurnal cycle corresponding to particulate emissions from vehicular traffic in Toronto [calculated from (87)]. Tracers are emitted at the lowest atmospheric level, 0 to 4 m. This methodology ignores sources emitting above 4 m as well as nonurban sources of pollution and processes that would remove or transform real pollutants, such as chemical and deposition processes; however, its purpose is to quantify key factors within the city that promote or inhibit urban dispersion.

We integrate the BEP-Tree model (47) into WRF-BEP-BEM to enable advanced simulation of the impacts of urban street tree planting. BEP-Tree is a modified version of BEP that simulates leaf stomatal opening and associated energy balances as well as radiative and hydrodynamic interactions between street trees and buildings, streets, and the atmosphere, and the resulting effects on local climate. Trees are integrated into the urban canopy, and their complete radiation, momentum, and heat exchanges are captured. BEP-Tree has been successfully evaluated against measurements at street and neighborhood scales from three cities (47). The inclusion of BEP-Tree in WRF is evaluated against street-level radiation and surface temperature measurements in London, Canada (SI Appendix, Supplementary Section 2).

4.2. Boundary Conditions.

Integrated Climate and Land Use Scenarios [ICLUS; (88)] data were used for WRF simulations in Phoenix and Miami. They provide land use/land cover (LU/LC) and impervious cover data consistent with each of the two climate change and urban development pathways we examine: RCP 4.5, a moderate emissions scenario; and RCP 8.5, a worst-case high emissions and population scenario. Thus, ICLUS provides different urban extent and density distributions for SoC and each projected EoC periods. For the Toronto simulations, we use the LCZ generator tool (89) for urban LU/LC (90) because ICLUS data are not available. For all cities, MODIS LU/LC data (91) are used for nonurban areas. The resulting urban density maps are visualized in SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5. We emphasize that Toronto EoC simulations do not reflect projected urban development while Miami and Phoenix simulations do. Thus, the neighborhoods analyzed in Phoenix and Miami may densify in future periods, increasing roof area and changing street area over which adaptation strategies act, and surrounding suburbs may expand, inducing advected effects. These urban development impacts are not included in the Toronto simulations. Any comparison between these cities in this study should bear this in mind.

SoC simulations are forced with ERA-Interim global reanalysis data (92). EoC simulations are forced with Community Earth Systems Model [CESM (85)] CMIP5 output that has been bias-corrected for all relevant variables using ERA-Interim data (84).

4.3. Street-Scale Heat Stress Modeling.

Assessment of heat stress requires quantification of the microscale exposure of the pedestrian to solar and longwave (terrestrial) radiation. Here, these exposures are provided by TUF-Pedestrian, a microscale model that simulates the thermal and radiative environment in urban canyons (45). TUF-Pedestrian calculates surface temperature variation across urban facets at the meter scale. It then simulates absorbed longwave and shortwave radiation on a pedestrian located within the urban canyon, enabling the calculation of mean radiant temperature (TMRT) at different locations in the urban canyon. Its detailed radiation physics capture direct shading of urban surfaces and pedestrians by trees, as well as the reduction of sky view factor due to trees. It assumes that leaf temperature is equal to the urban canyon air temperature for the purposes of calculating TMRT.

Top-of-domain meteorological forcing for TUF-Pedestrian simulations is extracted from WRF-BEP-BEM output at twice the building height. TMRT output from TUF-Pedestrian is combined with air temperature, humidity, and wind speed from WRF-BEP-BEM to calculate the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI (93)), which is a measure of pedestrian heat stress that includes a dynamic clothing model and assumes metabolic heat production associated with a walking pace of 4.4 km/h. In this study, we use the default UTCI thresholds for slight, strong, and extreme heat stress of 26 °C, 38 °C, and 46 °C, respectively.

4.4. Case Selection and Model Parameters.

To capture increases in intensity of extreme heat under climate change, we simulate one-year return period week-long extreme heat events (“heatwaves” henceforth) from the SoC and EoC. Extreme heat events are determined by selecting the seven consecutive days of each year with the highest mean UTCI calculated with GCM/reanalysis data for each city, assuming clear-sky conditions. Consistency of solar angles between SoC and EoC time periods is important to control for exposure to solar radiation. Thus, if a seven-day event falls more than one month from the summer solstice, we instead select an event with a similar average UTCI from within one month of the solstice (and from a different year). More details regarding case selection can be found in ref. 40.

Key urban parameters are summarized in SI Appendix, Table S1. Parameters for Phoenix follow ref. 7. Parameters for Toronto and Miami are chosen to match those for Phoenix or use default WRF settings, corresponding to data in refs. 48 and 94. The AC coefficient of performance is set to 3.5 for all cities, and the AC penetration rate is set to 100% in Phoenix and Miami, and 42% in Toronto (40).

We note that pedestrians in the TUF-Pedestrian model are placed 2 m from building walls, where sidewalks are expected for the case of minimal building setbacks. Pedestrians are placed in a total of four locations: on each side of a north–south street and on each side of an east–west street, midway from the intersections. Except where otherwise noted, all pedestrian heat stress results are an average of these four locations.

4.5. Simulation of Heat Adaptation Strategies.

In addition to the base case simulations, a total of five adaptation strategies are simulated, as well as two combinations of adaptation strategies (cool roofs + street trees; PV roofs + street trees). To bracket their potential effect, we simulate the following adaptation strategies by approximating their maximum potential extent and intensity and include select sensitivity simulations to assess future development of PV technology, and irrigation of trees (Phoenix only):

4.5.1. Street trees.

The effects of street trees are simulated at the mesoscale using the BEP-Tree model with WRF (Section 4.1). Subsequently, in TUF-Pedestrian, trees are placed in all street areas except for intersections, yielding a canyon tree cover that is consistent with the BEP-Tree configuration. In both BEP-Tree and TUF-Pedestrian, we set canyon-average tree canopy cover to 67% and canyon-average LAI to 2.63, the maximum potential LAI in Toronto (95), which corresponds to a citywide tree cover of 40%. It should also be noted that the cities studied here already have a certain degree of street tree cover, which is accounted for by default in WRF via the Noah soil-vegetation scheme and the natural fraction of each grid cell. Our purpose here is to assess the impacts of street tree increases, using the BEP-Tree scheme within WRF to capture the impacts of building-tree interaction. Thus, existing trees as represented by Noah are present in both base case and street tree planting simulations. Added street trees in BEP-Tree are assumed to be unirrigated, with soil moisture set to typical summertime values for each city (SI Appendix, Table S2). However, for Phoenix, we also consider irrigated trees in this study’s results, although we assume the trees planted in Phoenix are drought-tolerant and can access some soil water via deep roots.

4.5.2. Cool roofs.

For cool roof simulations, roof albedo is increased to 0.85 in WRF-BEP-BEM. This value approximates the maximum of commercially available roof coatings [(7); https://downloads.energystar.gov/bi/qplist/roofs_prod_list.pdf?8ddd-02cf] at present, but may be standard by the EoC. Also, with present technology, coatings can fade and soil, reducing their albedo unless they are regularly cleaned. We note that our base case simulations assume no implementation of cool roofs, whereas cool roofs are currently implemented on approximately 3.5% of buildings in Phoenix, for example (96). Therefore, as with other adaptation strategies we simulate, our results for cool roofs should be viewed as a maximum effect.

4.5.3. Reflective pavements.

For reflective pavement simulations, street albedo is increased to 0.40 in both WRF-BEP-BEM and TUF-Pedestrian. Schneider et al. (75) found that, at locations that were freshly painted, street albedo could reach 0.38, but often decreased to 0.20 to 0.30 due to wear. It is not implausible that emerging technologies could be more wear-resistant. Albedos exceeding 0.40 are considered here to be visually uncomfortable and are therefore not studied.

4.5.4. Photovoltaic roofs.

The WRF-BEP-BEM PV roof module (97) is applied. We simulate a 100% rooftop coverage of PV in each city. We assume an upward emissivity of 0.79 and a conversion efficiency of 30%. This efficiency is higher than any commercially available rooftop PV panels to the best of the authors’ knowledge, but engineers have made small-scale PV cells with efficiencies that are as high or higher (98), and it is not unreasonable to believe that they could be standard by the EoC. We also conduct simulations assuming a conversion efficiency of 15%, which is more typical of contemporary installations.

4.5.5. Green roofs.

The WRF-BEP-BEM green roof module (97) is applied. We simulate a 100% rooftop coverage of grass in each city. Roofs are irrigated between 10 PM and midnight each day for a total of 25 mm/week. While this level of irrigation may appear large for arid environments in particular, usage of graywater for this purpose is one potential solution. Base cases assume no implementation of green roofs.
